Preface

A “tesuji” is the best available means in a situation where the two sides’ stones confront each other, and “proper play” is nothing other than “the correct means.”

“Crude,” on the other hand, means “ordinary, run-of-the-mill,” and “crude play,” the counterpart to “proper play,” can fairly be called “the mistaken means.”

In games between professional players, scenes involving “the marvel of a tesuji” hardly ever appear. When high-level technique meets high-level technique and both sides are shaving away at each other, they kill off one another’s winning moves one at a time and never hand the opponent a position where there is such a thing as “the only move.”

In games between amateurs, however, we often see positions where a single tesuji

4

produces a large effect. In such positions, whether you can put a tesuji to use or let the chance slip away with a crude means is what divides victory from defeat.

Of course, not everyone can play only proper moves right from the start. With beginner- and intermediate-level technique, one’s moves inevitably tend to drift toward the crude. In the end we come back to the ordinary conclusion that there is no way forward but to polish your feel for the game through everyday training - but if you look through this book, we think you will come to understand to some degree what proper play really is.

There is a saying: “Practice beats study.” With an eye to visual impact in particular, we have centered the contents on shapes that can be applied in your own games.

We hope this book proves useful in helping readers improve their strength.

The Editorial Department, Monthly Go Study

I ran my eye over it: the shape was the one in Diagram 1.

Once again I reflected on the long training that professional players go through, and I was deeply moved.

S 4-dan got up from the seat without a word. The sight of S 4-dan walking out of the office, head tilted in puzzlement, still stays with me.

But S 4-dan left me with a valuable lesson.

To put it another way, the thought process of taking up the various possibilities starting from the easy, crude ones is something only an amateur can follow, and playing crude moves is a privilege granted precisely because one is an amateur.

This is of course not to say that crude moves are good. But when facing the endless variety of situations that arise on the board, one cannot ignore them. There is such a thing as a “crude good move.”

Shogi, too, has a proverb: “Finish the attack with crude moves.”

It may seem odd to tell you readers, who bought this book in search of what proper play is, “Do not neglect crude moves,” but perhaps the point is that one must not make light of a partial truth.

The late Kubomatsu Katsukiyo, called the great master of Kansai, who raised Hashimoto Utaro 9-dan (deceased) and Kitani Minoru 9-dan (deceased), is said to have left pupils with the teaching, “Start your thinking from moves that seem unlikely.” Among these “unlikely moves” we might also include resources that look crude, strong moves with ugly shapes, and the like.

After all, “tesuji” does not mean “a cool-looking move”; it means “the very best resource in that particular position and situation.”

(Nagano)

Afterword

Monthly Go Study was published out of Kansai from Showa 51 (1976) until Heisei 1 (1989).

Everyone who learns go, without exception, falls under its spell. Professional players are the ultimate example, but most people wish to improve without ever managing it. At the time, the Kansai Ki-in was out of the limelight. Professionals who were saintly people in everyday life quietly strove to master go and wished to pass it on to go fans. With a so-called go journalist acting as intermediary, this became a monthly magazine. The title was inspired by the phrase “School of Go.”

As head of Gogakusha, I was responsible for the magazine’s finances, but they were difficult from the start and the burden grew year by year. I poured all my energy into the editing, but by the end of the Showa era I was utterly exhausted. A few years later, I lost every back issue in the Great Hanshin Earthquake.

More than ten years after the final issue, readers still write in with inquiries and tell me how much they miss Monthly Go Study. No editor could ask for more.

Miki Tadashi, Gogakusha Co., Ltd.
