How Emphasizing the Corner Changes the Fighting at the Star Point

When a stone is placed on the star point, the key question is how to handle the aji that remains in the corner. Modern evaluation tends to say that "the corner is highly valuable," so the points to emphasize differ between the player who takes the star point and the player facing it.

【For the player who takes the star point】 The aim is to seize the initiative skillfully and defend the corner, or to find efficient defensive techniques, offsetting the drawback of the aji left behind there.

【For the player facing the star point】 The crucial thing is not to miss the right moment to disrupt the corner. An approach move is often ignored, so invading at the 3-3 immediately has become the mainstream way to play.

In this chapter we have collected the representative joseki and techniques worth knowing from either side. Even the AlphaGo techniques are out of date nowadays. Prepare in advance and get ahead of your rivals!

Column 1

For those who can’t set aside much time to study Go, we recommend the "five-minute daily game-record check." We live in an age when professionals play online every day, so if you can make good use of the opportunity, you will find new techniques faster than anyone else. All you need to prepare is a device that can display online games and an internet connection.

① Look for game records on an online Go server.

② Take a screenshot of anything that strikes you as interesting or unusual.

③ When you have time, examine it with a Go AI or something similar.

Those three steps are all there is to it. It is fine to look only at the first 50 moves of the opening. This way each record takes about ten seconds, so in five minutes you can check a great many of them.

The key is to "record the positions your intuition finds interesting." You don’t need to grasp the meaning of the moves right away. Simply seeing new shapes and tesuji is good stimulation, and once you want to use them in your own games you will naturally start studying them, gradually building a productive loop of learning.

The Pros and Cons of Learning from Videos

Nowadays there are more and more chances to learn from videos on YouTube and elsewhere, so as long as you have an internet connection, anyone has an environment in which they can study at a high level. However, you do need to understand the pros and cons of studying with videos.

  • Pro: you can understand concrete methods visually, without having to reproduce the sequence in your head.

  • Con: concrete methods are tools, and yet they become the goal.

Most video sites are built so that users can watch for free, funded by advertising revenue, so the creators too find ways to draw viewers in, using inflammatory phrases and expressions that push right up to the line of what a broad interpretation will allow. Superfluous variations are trimmed away in order to lead to a single conclusion, which makes the opinion look correct; the result is that you are given no room to think while knowledge is hammered in one item after another.

The thing to watch out for when learning from videos is that what you are getting is no more than one way of thinking. It is important to always leave yourself "margin" for taking in a variety of ideas. Since wringing out a move at the board is difficult, broadening the range of your ideas with videos and the like should lead to a higher winning percentage.

Why Some People Can Lay Out a Long Joseki in an Instant

It has long been said that "there is no need to memorize joseki" and "memorize joseki, then forget them." For example, even a joseki with a long sequence is, in most cases, nothing more than a skillful combination of basic tesuji once you break it down into small pieces. But the moment it is condensed into a single variation, it suddenly feels difficult.

The plan for solving this problem is the method of "solving tsumego over and over." More than training your reading, the point is to make the patterns of reading part of your body. A single tesuji is often one set of a few moves, and once you have grasped those patterns, you can then optimize your reading by combining them.

I expect you have often seen a strong player lay out a variation dozens of moves long in an instant. In fact, even a long variation can in most cases be explained in three or four patterns once you have grasped the basic ones, and being able to reproduce it with what amounts to about three moves of reading is the trick behind the magic.

“The Sacrifice Mindset” - It Changes Once You Put It into Practice

One of the reasons your strength stops improving is "saving too many stones." Everyone must have had the experience at least once of ending up with so many burdensome stones that the position got out of hand. Of course, training your reading and building up your fighting power is one solution. But the fundamental solution is telling apart "the stones you should save and the stones you should sacrifice."

Concretely, it means "comparing the stones you want to capture with the stones that will be captured." Simple as that sounds, in actual games most players push ahead with the greedy idea of capturing the opponent’s stones and saving their own as well. As a result they defeat themselves, and because their review focuses on the local fight, they never reach the fundamental solution and repeat the same mistake.

Conversely, the better players are at fighting, the more they bring their full concentration to the moment they launch an attack. The point is to leave the opponent no room for choice and draw them into a one-sided fight. If you are the attacker you can seize the initiative in the fight, and if you are defending, you have already decided which stones to save, so no hesitation arises. Even high-dan players often fight by brute force, so if you can acquire "the mindset of sacrificing," you can get ahead of your rivals.
