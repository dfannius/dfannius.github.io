Preface

The theme of this book is the sacrifice stone. If anything, rather than introducing sacrifice-stone tesujis, its aim is to get you thinking about the very idea of "giving up stones." It is true that kyu-level players seem unable to let stones go, and end up running with stones that one would never dream of trying to save. You see such situations all the time.

We professionals never even entertain the idea of running away with a stone that ought to be sacrificed, so it had never occurred to us to ask why sacrificing stones is a good thing in the first place.

And so, in preparing this book, I tried to think the matter through from the ground up, starting with the question "why do we sacrifice stones?" I touch on the answer in the introductory chapter as well, but it comes down to a single line: "we sacrifice stones because giving them up is the more profitable course." Well then, why is it more profitable to sacrifice a stone? And what kind of stone is one that you must never give up?

I have done my very best, in my own way, to think through how to tell which stones should be sacrificed and to explain it here, and I would be glad if it proves even a little useful to you all.

One more note: please treat the examples drawn from amateur games as basic sacrifice-stone principles aimed at kyu-level players, and those drawn from my own games as applied problems aimed at dan-level players.

August 2010

Mimura Tomoyasu

What Is a Sacrifice Stone?
8
Why Do We Sacrifice Stones?

Before we study sacrifice stones, there is a question we must settle first. Quite simply, it is this: "Why do we sacrifice stones in the first place?"

There is only one answer. It may be obvious, but it is: "because giving up the stones is the more profitable course."

To take an extreme example, consider the shape in Diagram 1. When White gives atari at 1, you all play Black 2 as in Diagram 2 and sacrifice the three marked stones, don’t you? If instead you save the marked stones with Black 1 as in Diagram 3 and let White push through at 2, the damage is enormous.

The difference between Diagrams 2 and 3 is perfectly clear. Yet when I look closely at your own games, there is no shortage of positions where sacrificing stones would gain you even more than in this example.

Keep firmly in mind that "there are times when giving up stones is the more profitable choice."

9

It is easy enough to say "giving up stones is profitable," but there are actually several types of sacrifice stone.

① Sacrifice stones you don’t need. ② Sacrifice stones that can’t be saved. ③ By giving up stones, you improve the shape of your own stones, or you gain the chance to play several moves on the outside.

Pattern ③ is what we call the "squeeze" tesuji. Famous sacrifice-stone proverbs such as "make it two stones, then sacrifice them" fall under ③, and the general image people have of a sacrifice stone probably refers to the ③ type.

Before you learn such sacrifice-stone techniques, however, you must first practice sizing up "the value of stones." What you need in amateur Go is not to master advanced sacrifice-stone techniques.

What matters more is to stop running with unneeded stones without a second thought — to stop running with stones that can’t be saved, or stones that gain you nothing even if you do save them, only to pile up further losses. This is the first step in sacrificing stones. To do that, it is important to build the habit of "telling apart the stones you need from the stones you don’t."

Then, once you recognize that a stone is unnecessary for you, let your opponent capture it without regret. Once you’re able to do this much, you can say you have mastered the basics of sacrificing stones.

Now let us actually look at the basic shapes of these three patterns.

The Squeeze

Finally, there are the sacrifice stones of the so-called "squeeze." By letting your opponent capture your stones, you can play any number of moves on the outside, obtain good shape, or take sente.

Now, at last, with Chapter 1 we begin practical training in sacrifice stones. First, though, amateur players should start with their mindset and throw away any "allergy to sacrificing stones."

Go is not a game of capturing stones. Having your stones captured doesn’t mean you lose. On the contrary, some stones are strategically unnecessary, and there are positions where sacrificing a stone that threatens to become a burden makes things much easier. First, please understand that "there are stones you don’t need." Learning the tesuji and other techniques for sacrificing stones comes at the next stage.

At the same time, it isn’t as though sacrificing every stone under the sun is good, either. Even a single stone can be a "stone you must not sacrifice." For example, Diagram 28 shows the sort of dubious "sacrifice" that amateurs often play, but it lets White capture a ponnuki, and Black suffers a big loss.

There are stones — even several at once — that are better sacrificed, and there are single stones that must never be sacrificed. Let’s start with practice in telling apart "stones you must not sacrifice" and "stones you’d do better to sacrifice."

"Training your eye" is important in Go, but it is especially important when you are deciding whether to sacrifice stones. Read this book, and you will soon come to understand how to judge the size of stones.

Chapter 1
Unneeded Stones ①
— Stones Stuck to Strong Stones —
20

Put into words, "sacrifice the stones you don’t need" sounds simple. But which stones are the ones you don’t need?

There are various ways to describe an unneeded stone. You could call it a "stone with no work to do," or rephrase it as a "stone that has served its purpose." If it were easy to tell whether a stone still has work to do or whether its role is finished, no one would have any trouble in the first place.

In this book, I break things down in a somewhat more practical way, centering the discussion on these two points:

① Stones Stuck to Strong Stones ② Forcing Stones (Stones with No Follow-Up Threat)

My aim is to help you develop the ability to tell whether a given stone is necessary or one you don’t need.

In Chapter 1, of the two above, you’ll thoroughly master the most important one: "stones stuck to strong stones."

The forcing stones and stones with no follow-up threat that you’ll study in Chapter 2 are, in most cases, also stuck to the opponent’s strong stones.

Also, when you’re deciding whether to sacrifice a stone, you must not look only at your own stones. If you fixate on your own stone count alone and tell yourself, "I could still give up one stone, but giving up three or more is unthinkable," throw away that kind of assumption right now.

A stone that has become stuck to the opponent’s strong stones gains nothing at all by running — it will only be attacked severely.

The first step in sacrificing stones is not memorizing tesuji. What matters is to calmly judge the strength of the opponent’s stones against your own; if the opponent’s stones look much stronger, sacrifice your stones without regret and settle the situation while the damage is still small.

86
Don’t Be Fooled by the Number of Stones

In Chapters 1 and 2 you looked at a great many theme diagrams. I hope you have gotten a reasonable grasp of what an "unneeded stone" is.

If you save an unneeded stone that is stuck to your opponent’s strong stones, you damage your position in no time at all. While you are running, the stones grow bigger and bigger and become harder to sacrifice, and it often happens that before you know it you have fallen into a losing position with no way to break through. If you sacrifice them early, though, you can then play the other big points with no constraints and nothing to worry about.

Now, in this chapter I have taken a slightly different approach and gone looking through my own games for ones I think you may find interesting. I hope you will enjoy them at your leisure, as both a review of Chapters 1 and 2 and a break. Please look at how a professional, having judged stones to be "unneeded stones," decisively sacrifices them without regard to the number of stones.

Some of these are games where things became so painful that there was nothing to do but sacrifice, but as long as weak stones keep running, they have to keep being attacked. However, no matter how large a group is, once you have made up your mind to sacrifice it, it can no longer be attacked. On the contrary, by letting the opponent capture it you become able to aim at all sorts of aji.

Of course, if a truly large group is captured, there are also times when no room is left for a comeback.

Even so, as in the games introduced in this chapter, boldly letting a large group be captured and then breaking the position open by playing the other big points one after another can feel even better than capturing the opponent’s large group.

As a way to escape "the pain of being attacked" as quickly as possible, I recommend keeping "sacrifice stones" among your options.

104
Recognizing Stones You Can’t Give Up

Up to this point I have centered the discussion on the idea that "unneeded stones and small stones" should be sacrificed right away. The knack for getting good at sacrifice is to build the habit of always judging the size of your stones, and:

  • Don’t reflexively connect when you are put in atari
  • Think about sacrificing stones

It hardly needs saying by now how important it is to observe these two.

However, in order to understand unneeded stones correctly, you also have to become able to recognize "necessary stones". Conversely to the case of unneeded stones, there are also stones that you must not let your opponent capture, not even a single one.

From this chapter on, by studying "stones you must not give up", you will acquire the ability to sacrifice stones correctly.

  • Stones that are fine to sacrifice
  • Stones you must not give up

If you can tell these two apart, then even without knowing the finer techniques of sacrifice, you should see considerable results in your own games.

Here are some criteria for deciding whether a stone is important:

① Stones that cut the opponent’s stones ② Stones that threaten the opponent’s base ③ Stones that let you leave threats and cutting points in the opponent’s position ④ Simply, stones that are big when captured (a ponnuki, for example)

Stones That Leave Cutting Points or Threats

Even stones that do not directly cut the opponent or threaten their base must not be given up lightly, if saving them lets you leave a threat behind for later.

The stones in Chapters 4 and 5 are ones I would like you to be able to save without a moment’s thought. If anything, it is in this chapter that you will find yourself unsure whether to save or to sacrifice.

Stones that leave a threat against the opponent are, so to speak, one stage short of the stones in Chapters 4 and 5. Even so, if they are captured the efficiency of the opponent’s stones improves, so you must not let them be taken lightly.

The theme of this chapter is "stones that leave a threat against the opponent’s stones," but in actual games almost all of them are cutting points.

Interestingly, as in Theme 4 on page 167 later on, there is a perfectly good way of playing that goes: "run once in order to leave a cutting point behind, and then simply sacrifice the stones once the opponent defends the cutting point." That same Theme 4 example can also be looked at, as I will show in the next chapter, Chapter 7, in terms of the idea that "being captured in a ponnuki is very big, so run just the one time."

It also happens that the very same stone, one that should have been saved, becomes a stone that should be sacrificed after just one more move. This may sound confusing, but in essence it is only a question of "sacrifice the stone or save it," so if you feel you do not need it, sacrifice it, and if you feel you do, save it.

The purpose of this book is, above all, to get you to hold on to the idea of "giving up stones." And then, so that you can judge correctly when to give a stone up, to have you acquire the guidelines for doing so.

At last we come to the final chapter on stones you must not give up. The theme of this chapter is "stones that are big if captured." Every stone you must not give up is a big stone, but up to now the discussion has centered on "the quality of stones." Chapter 8
The Squeeze and Other Tesujis
186
Sacrifice Stones for the Squeeze

Now then, have you managed to get a good feel for telling apart the stones you can afford to sacrifice from the stones you must not sacrifice?

From this chapter on, I will talk about sacrifice-stone technique, which is one level above what we have covered so far.

A sacrifice stone can have all sorts of purposes — putting the opponent in shortage of liberties, forcing them into bad shape, taking away their eye shape, and so on — but if anything, those mostly belong to the world of tesuji and life-and-death problems.

In this chapter I would like to focus on the "squeeze," which is a more practical sacrifice-stone technique. Why is it profitable to use a sacrifice stone to squeeze your opponent? Understanding this is the first step toward using sacrifice stones well.

The most important point is that by sacrificing a stone, you worsen the work the opponent’s stones are doing. Even if one of your own stones is captured, if the opponent’s stones end up working two moves’ worth less, that is a huge profit.

The knack for worsening the work of the opponent’s stones is to keep the exchange of "inside for outside" in mind. A shape in which the opponent’s stones huddle inside for move after move while your own stones play around the outside is one where your stones are working well.

Chapter 9
Sacrifice or Save? — Practice Problems
234
Next-Move Problems from Game Positions

In this chapter I have prepared a number of problems based on positions from actual games.

Up to now, you have studied the basic ways of thinking: what sort of stones are unnecessary, what sort of stones must not be sacrificed, and so on.

Please use that thinking to decide, in a variety of situations, whether to sacrifice a stone or save it. Better still, go on to consider how to sacrifice it or how to save it, and picture the continuation that follows.

The material comes from amateur games and from my own games, but the basic ideas I have discussed so far come up again and again, so please use these problems to review and deepen your understanding.

Also, get into the habit of thinking not only from the standpoint of the player sacrificing stones but from the standpoint of the player capturing them. Since it is effective to let your opponent capture unnecessary stones, looking at it from the other side, it follows that "you should not capture unnecessary stones." There may well be situations where you should deliberately refrain from capturing stones you could capture, and resist instead.

In a real game, whether you capture or not, you must always weigh how big the stones are. In most cases the ideas you have learned so far apply directly, but if you do not look at the whole position carefully before judging, you will sometimes go wrong. What matters is forming the habit of "thinking."

Saving or sacrificing stones vaguely, without thinking at all, will not lead to improvement. Even if you get it wrong, having thought about whether to sacrifice or save will always pay off later.

Please take on this chapter as a guide to how to think in real game situations.

Afterword

Thank you for reading this book all the way to the end.

As the saying goes, "once you can sacrifice stones, you are a dan-level player," and there is no doubt that acquiring a feel for sacrifice stones is a major key to improving at go.

The first step with sacrifice stones is to have the idea of "giving up stones" in the first place. If you reflexively run away with a stone that is in atari, or save unnecessary stones without thinking, you cannot even begin to study sacrifice stones.

The next step is to judge accurately which stones can be given up. Once you understand that, you will be able to become a dan-level player.

Squeeze tesuji, and tesuji that make sabaki by giving up a large group, come after that. Once you have those, you will unquestionably have reached the level of a high-dan player.

If this book becomes the occasion for you to master sacrifice stones, nothing could give me greater pleasure.
